The yearbook walkthrough

BLAST + HMMER for EnzymeX  ·  how the analogy maps onto the code
The whole product in one picture. You have a yearbook with 273,000 people in it. Each person's photo is a protein sequence; each person's job title is their EC number. Someone walks in with a photo of a stranger and asks what that person does for a living. You don't know — but if you find the people in the yearbook who look most like them, and they're all plumbers, that is real evidence. Everything below is the machinery for answering that question fast, and for being honest about how good the answer is.

Part 1 — Preparing the yearbook (offline, minutes, on a schedule)

What happensModuleDetail worth knowing
1Go get the photosreferences/db.py Opens the warehouse once, read-only, reads in primary-key order, then closes it. This is the only database connection in the entire system.
2Throw out the junkreferences/export.py Drops sequences under 30 letters, over 10,000, more than 10% unreadable letters, and exact duplicates. Every rejection is written down with a reason — a silent export is a useless export.
Source is how much you trust the job title on the back of the photo. swissprot: a human curator read the paper and wrote the EC down. pdb: someone solved the 3D structure, so the photo is real, but the entry is whatever was in the crystal — which is where the group-photo problem comes from. trembl: a computer guessed the label and nobody checked. kegg: EC inherited from elsewhere. The first two are kept, the last two dropped.
3Type up the name cardsreferences/metadata.py Photo id → EC number, source, description, in a single local file. This is why a search can label a hit without ever reopening MySQL.
4Make the photos fast to findreferences/blast_build.py Hands the pile to makeblastdb. Turns “compare against 273,000 photos” from minutes into milliseconds.
5Sort people into familiesreferences/cluster.py MMseqs2 groups by who actually looks alike — 35% of letters matching across 80% of both. Families too small or too ragged to blend are kicked out and logged.
6Blend each family into one portraitreferences/hmmer_build.py MAFFT lines the family up, hmmbuild blends them into a composite where shared features come out sharp and variable ones blurry, hmmpress makes it fast to read. That composite is a profile HMM.
7Stamp the edition numberreferences/manifest.py Fingerprints the sequences plus the settings that decide what is searchable. Same data and settings → same id, so the build is reproducible; change anything → new id, automatically.
Why sort by looks and not by job title? Because plumbers don't look alike. Blending “everyone whose job is plumber” gives grey mush that vaguely resembles everybody and identifies nobody. An EC number names a reaction, not a family: glutathione transferase is one EC spread across 13 families that look nothing like each other. So cluster by appearance first, then check which job titles landed in each family and report that purity instead of assuming it.
Whose schedule? Theirs, and it already exists. EnzymeX runs a quarterly cron that pulls from Swiss-Prot, PDB, KEGG and TrEMBL and refills enzymesdata — that is the warehouse being restocked, and it has nothing to do with this code. The build just hangs off it: warehouse restocks → reprint the yearbook → a new edition number falls out on its own. Roughly four times a year, four minutes each time.

Part 2 — Someone walks in (online, seconds, per request)

What happensModuleDetail worth knowing
1Check it's a real photofasta.py Right letters, long enough, not a strip of DNA pasted by mistake. Bad input is refused before anything expensive starts.
2Open a folder for this visitjobs.py Everything about this one search goes in it: the query, what each tool printed on stdout and stderr, and the parsed result. Nothing is discarded, so any past search can be reopened and explained.
3Ask the same question three wayssearch/blast.py
search/hmmer.py		 blastp, phmmer and hmmscan run as separate programs with a timeout, reading only the files from Part 1. No database is opened.
4Read back what they saidsearch/parsers.py
search/outcome.py		 Tool output is turned into rows and checked. “Found nothing”, “crashed” and “timed out” are kept as three different answers, so a failure never renders as an empty table.
5Write the names on and show itsearch/service.py
web/routes.py		 Each hit comes back as a bare id; it's looked up in the name-card box for its EC, source and description, then rendered as three tables plus CSV and JSON.

The three searches

ToolWhat it does in the yearbookWhat that buys you
blastpHolds the photo next to every single yearbook photo, one at a time, and reports the closest ones with a percentage.Fast and precise, and the only one that gives a percent identity. Only catches people who genuinely look alike.
phmmerWorks out which features actually matter and weighs those, instead of comparing flat.Catches distant cousins that blunt comparison misses. Slower, and it cannot report a percent identity.
hmmscanIgnores individuals and compares against the family portraits instead.Answers “which family are you in”, which is useful when no single person is a good match but a family clearly is.
The three results are shown as three separate tables and are never merged into one ranked list. Each tool scores on its own scale, so combining them would be like ranking runners by mixing seconds with miles per hour.
Only hmmscan uses the families. phmmer builds a throwaway portrait from your submitted sequence on the spot and compares that against every individual in the book — no families involved. And nothing upstream hands you families: the warehouse gives you loose photos and nothing else. MMseqs2 works out who looks alike during the build. Of 201 groups found, 47 were big and consistent enough to be worth a portrait.

Where the output actually lives

WhereWhat is in it
/jobs/<id>The results page. Submitting redirects here automatically. Three tables, plus a grey strip at the top carrying the job id, the build id and the download links.
/jobs/<id>/results.csv
/jobs/<id>/results.json		The same data, machine-readable. One row per hit; a method that found nothing or failed still emits a row carrying its status, so “searched, found nothing” stays distinguishable from “never ran”.
var/jobs/<id>/On disk: query.fasta, blastp_hits.tsv, phmmer_tblout.txt, phmmer_domtblout.txt, hmmscan_tblout.txt, hmmscan_domtblout.txt, the *_full.txt human-readable versions, every *.stderr.txt, and results.json.
The raw formats. outfmt 6 is BLAST's tab-separated table with no headers; the 13 columns are chosen, not default. --tblout is HMMER's one-row-per-sequence, --domtblout its one-row-per-region. Four of the BLAST columns — qlen slen sstart send — are requested specifically because subject coverage is computed from them. All three formats are then retyped onto one common shape (hit id, EC, identity, E-value, bit score, coverage) and kept in three separate tables.
To show it in 40 seconds: raw blastp_hits.tsv → normalized results.json → the rendered page.

Where this sits next to the models

  • ECPICK, HIT-EC, CLEAN are models. They learned patterns and will return an EC for any sequence, including one with no known relative anywhere. They always answer, and you cannot tell which answers to doubt.
  • This is a lookup. It only answers if something in the book resembles you, it can say nothing, and it always shows its work: who matched, how closely, and over how much of each sequence.
The honest framing is not “more accurate”. It is: the models can predict where this cannot; when this answers, the answer is checkable. Which is why the results page says, in its own words, that these tables sit next to ECPICK, HIT-EC and CLEAN rather than replacing them.

Two things this design buys, and one trap it catches

  • The site can't be dragged down by the database. Searches read files. If MySQL is busy or down, search still answers — I have run it in exactly that state.
  • Every answer is attributable. The edition number is on the result page, so six months later you can say precisely which snapshot produced it.
  • The GFP trap. GFP is not an enzyme, but it comes back with 25 hits at up to 97.5% identity, because PDB is full of proteins glued to GFP to make them visible. 15 of those carry an EC number and none of them are GFP's.
  • How it's caught: the match covers 100% of the query but only 55% of the subject. It matched a piece. BLAST does not report that number, so it is computed here from merged match intervals.

If you remember nothing else

Build the yearbook once, offline, and stamp it with an edition number. Then every search reads only that yearbook, three different ways, and never touches the database at all.
